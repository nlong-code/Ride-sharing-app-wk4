Our group approach

How We Work Together

We are developing the application in stages. Each weekly update builds on the work from the week before it. Team members take the lead in their assigned areas, then the group reviews how the pieces fit together.

1. Identify the need

We start with the people who will use or support the app. We discuss what passengers, drivers, and administrators need the system to do.

2. Agree on requirements

We turn those needs into functional and non-functional requirements. These requirements give our group a shared direction for the design.

3. Model the system

We use use case diagrams, activity diagrams, context diagrams, architecture views, and DFDs to explain how the system and its users exchange information.

4. Design and review

We develop interface mockups, database entities, relationships, and a data dictionary. We compare these items with the requirements and revise anything that does not line up.

How we coordinate the work

Darius's project coordination helps the group keep track of deadlines and review progress. Norma's requirements work helps us check that design choices connect to stakeholder needs. Yusuke's modeling work helps show the system processes visually. These areas overlap, so our diagrams, written explanations, and design choices should be reviewed as a team before we treat them as final.

For each weekly submission, our group can check that the written explanation matches the diagrams, that the diagrams use the same process names, and that the design still supports the requirements we identified earlier. If the group changes a requirement, we should also check whether the DFD, ERD, interface mockups, or data dictionary need to be updated.

Proposed application workflow

How Our Ride-Sharing App Is Intended to Work

The following walkthrough brings the group's Week 2 requirements and activity-diagram descriptions together. It explains the planned passenger and driver experience from beginning to end.

  1. Account access: A passenger or driver creates an account or logs in. The system checks the account information and gives the user access to the appropriate features.
  2. Passenger enters trip details: The passenger enters a pickup location and destination. The app uses mapping/GPS information to help estimate the route, distance, and travel time.
  3. Fare is shown before booking: The app displays an upfront fare breakdown. The passenger can confirm the request or cancel it. If the passenger changes the destination or adds a stop, the app calculates a revised fare and asks for approval.
  4. Available drivers are contacted: After the passenger confirms, the app searches for an available driver and sends the ride details. The driver can accept or decline the request.
  5. The passenger receives the result: If a driver accepts, the passenger receives confirmation and can track the driver's location and estimated arrival. If the driver declines, the app continues looking for another available driver.
  6. The trip is completed: The driver updates the trip status as the ride progresses and marks it completed at the destination.
  7. Payment and feedback: The app sends the agreed fare to the external payment processor. The passenger receives payment confirmation or a notice if payment fails, with an option to retry or request support. After payment is confirmed, the passenger can receive a receipt, rate the trip, or report an issue.
Important design rule: The fare agreed to before the ride should remain fixed unless the passenger changes the trip and approves a revised price. The app coordinates payment through a third-party payment processor; it does not need to store raw card numbers or security codes.
Week 1 · Project initiation and requirements gathering

Week 1: Project Initiation

1. Stakeholder Analysis

Our group identified passengers and drivers as the primary stakeholders. We considered what passengers need to request rides, understand the fare, and receive trip updates. We also considered what drivers need to review ride requests, see pickup and destination information, accept or decline rides, and monitor earnings. We included administrators and external services such as mapping/GPS and payment processing because they also interact with or support the system.

2. Initial Requirements Gathering

As a group, we began translating stakeholder needs into requirements. Our initial list includes account registration, ride requests, pickup and destination selection, upfront fare estimates, trip updates, payment processing, ratings, ride history, driver earnings, and customer support. We also identified security, accessibility, reliability, performance, ease of use, and data privacy as important quality expectations. AI-assisted fare predictions were discussed as a possible idea, but we left them optional until the group can decide whether they fit the project scope.

3. Project Charter

Our group's goal is to design a ride-sharing application that connects passengers and drivers through a reliable and easy-to-use platform. Together, we are documenting requirements, modeling the main processes, planning the data structure, and developing interface concepts. We are giving special attention to upfront pricing, trip visibility, privacy, and secure payment handling.

Week 2 · System analysis artifacts

Week 2: System Analysis

1. Functional and Non-Functional Requirements

Our team continued developing the requirements using the stakeholder needs identified earlier. The Week 2 update records 11 functional requirements and 6 non-functional requirements. Together, these cover account registration, pickup and destination details, upfront fare pricing, ride requests, driver acceptance, trip tracking, payments, ratings and support, ride history, driver earnings, administrator functions, security, usability, reliability, accessibility, performance, and data privacy. We treated these as the working requirements to guide the diagrams and design.

2. Use Case Diagrams

Our use case work identifies passengers, drivers, and system administrators as the main actors. We mapped the actions each actor needs to perform, including requesting rides, accepting or declining requests, making payments, viewing trip history, managing accounts, and reviewing reported issues. The use case model gives the group a shared picture of who uses each part of the system.

3. Activity Diagrams

Our group described three main activity flows: a passenger requests a ride, a driver accepts or declines the request, and a trip is completed with payment processing. We included the decisions that affect the next step. For example, if a driver declines, the system continues searching; if payment fails, the passenger is notified and can retry or request support.

4. Begin System Context Diagram

We began the context diagram by placing the Ride-Sharing App at the center and identifying the external entities that exchange information with it: passengers, drivers, mapping/GPS services, payment processing services, and system administrators. This gives our group a clear system boundary before breaking the application into smaller processes.

Our group should keep the original draw.io diagrams with the written explanation when the assignment requires the visual artifacts. The explanation here describes the purpose of each model; it does not replace an original diagram.
Week 3 · High-level design artifacts

Week 3: High-Level Design

1. System Context Diagram

We carried the Week 2 context diagram into the Week 3 design work and refined how the main users and external services connect to the application. As a group, we use this diagram to check that the system boundary includes passenger and driver activity as well as mapping and payment services.

2. High-Level Architecture Diagram

Our high-level architecture organizes the proposed system into user interfaces, an application/server layer, core services, data storage, and external integrations. The passenger and driver interfaces connect to application functions such as account management, ride management, and payment coordination. The group uses this view to explain how the major parts fit together without selecting a specific technology stack yet.

3. Initial Data Flow Diagrams

Our group developed context-level and Level 1 data flow views to show how information moves through the application. The Level 1 processes cover account management, ride requests and fare estimates, driver matching and trip tracking, payment coordination, and ratings or issue reporting. We use these diagrams to connect the requirements to the information the system receives, processes, stores, and sends to external services.

4. User Interface Mockups

We began translating the requirements into passenger and driver screen concepts. The passenger mockups focus on entering pickup and destination details, reviewing and approving the upfront fare, and tracking an assigned driver. The driver concepts focus on receiving a ride request and deciding whether to accept or decline. These screens help the group check whether the planned functions are understandable from each user's point of view.

Week 4 · Detailed design artifacts

Week 4: Detailed Design

During Week 4, our group builds on the Week 3 design work and adds more detail. We are checking how information moves through the app, how database records connect, what passengers and drivers see on their screens, and how the main data fields should be defined. These items need to stay consistent with the requirements and process models we already developed.

1. Complete Data Flow Diagrams

Our detailed DFD expands the processes we identified in Week 3. It shows how passenger and driver information enters the system, how ride requests are handled, how drivers are matched, how trip progress is tracked, and how payment results return from the external processor. As a group, we use the DFD to check that each major process has the information it needs and that the data stores support the work.

Passenger input

Account information, pickup location, destination, and ride confirmation.

Driver matching

Checks available drivers and sends ride request details to a driver.

External services

Mapping/GPS supports location information; the payment provider processes payment.

Data storage

Stores account, ride, driver assignment, and payment status records.

DFD notation note: rectangles represent external entities, rounded process boxes represent system processes, and the database represents stored information. In a formal DFD, each arrow should be labeled with the exact data flow it carries.

Driver — Ride

Ride — Payment

Location — Ride

Database design decision to confirm: This draft uses one payment record per ride and allows a ride to have a driver assignment after matching. The group should confirm whether it needs to support multiple payment attempts or unassigned rides before treating this as final.
