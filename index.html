Proposed application workflow

How Our Ride-Sharing App Is Intended to Work

The following walkthrough brings the group's Week 2 requirements and activity-diagram descriptions together. This diagram explains the planned passenger and driver experience from beginning to end.

  1. Account access:A passenger or driver has to create an account for themselves, and log in. The app will verify their account and give them full access to all of the apps features.
  2. Passenger enters trip details:The passenger enters a pick up location and where they want to be dropped off. The app provides a possible route, estimates how long it should take to get from point A to point B, and an estimate of the total miles traveled.
  3. Fare is shown before booking:The app shows the estimated cost of the ride before you book your ride. The passenger may confirm their booking or cancel their booking. Should the passenger decide to go somewhere else than what was originally booked, the passenger will have to approve a new quote based on the changed drop-off location and/or added stops.
  4. Available drivers are contacted:Once you have approved your booking, the app looks for a driver that is currently available. Once an available driver is found, the app will send the driver the complete details of your ride. The driver then either approves or rejects the ride.
  5. The passenger receives the result:If the driver approves your ride, the passenger will receive a notification confirming acceptance and providing the ability to view the location of their driver and an estimated time of arrival. If the driver rejects your ride, the app will continue searching for another available driver.
  6. The trip is completed:As the ride is progressing (i.e., as the driver reaches each stop) the driver can update the trip status, finally marking it as completed when arriving at the final destination.
  7. Payment and feedback:After receiving confirmation that the agreed upon amount has been sent through to the external payment processing company; the passenger will receive notification if payment was made successfully or if there were issues making payment. The passenger will also receive an opportunity to retry payment or contact customer service. Once payment is confirmed, the passenger will have access to a receipt, rating options for their trip experience, and reporting options should something happen during/after their trip.
Important design rule: Unless you are changing your trip and approve of an amended fare, the original fare which is determined prior to the start of your trip will be your charge for the ride. The app coordinates payment through a third-party payment processor; it does not need to store raw card numbers or security codes.
Week 1 · Project initiation and requirements gathering

Week 1: Project Initiation

1. Stakeholder Analysis

Our group identified passengers and drivers as the primary stakeholders. We considered what passengers need to request rides, understand the fare, and receive trip updates. We also considered what drivers need to review ride requests, see pickup and destination information, accept or decline rides, and monitor earnings. We included administrators and external services such as mapping/GPS and payment processing because they also interact with or support the system.

2. Initial Requirements Gathering

As a group, we began translating stakeholder needs into requirements. Our initial list includes account registration, ride requests, pickup and destination selection, upfront fare estimates, trip updates, payment processing, ratings, ride history, driver earnings, and customer support. We also identified security, accessibility, reliability, performance, ease of use, and data privacy as important quality expectations. AI-assisted fare predictions were discussed as a possible idea, but we left them optional until the group can decide whether they fit the project scope.

3. Project Charter

Our group's goal is to design a ride-sharing application that connects passengers and drivers through a reliable and easy-to-use platform. Together, we are documenting requirements, modeling the main processes, planning the data structure, and developing interface concepts. We are giving special attention to upfront pricing, trip visibility, privacy, and secure payment handling.

Week 2 · System analysis artifacts

Week 2: System Analysis

1. Functional and Non-Functional Requirements

Our team began to develop the requirements for the project based on the stakeholder needs we had previously determined. We recorded 11 functional requirements and 6 nonfunctional in our week two updates. All of the above functional and nonfunctional requirements (total) include account registration, pickup and drop off information; a price quote prior to accepting an actual fare; requesting a ride; a driver accepting or declining a request; the ability to track your trips; payment processing; rating drivers, customers, etc.; customer service options and a support area; viewing a ride history; how much money a driver is making per month/week/etc; what admin can do with their functions for this application; Security, usability, reliability, accessibility, performance, and Data Privacy.

2. Use Case Diagrams

We identified our primary users (passengers, drivers, and system administrators) through our use-case development process. Through this development we also mapped out what each user must do when using our application (i.e. requesting a ride; accept/deny the request; make payment; view your trip history; manage your account; review any issues you may have with your ride). We identified our primary users (passengers, drivers, and system administrators) through our use-case development process. Through this development we also mapped out what each user must do when using our application (i.e. requesting a ride; accept/deny the request; make payment; view your trip history; manage your account; review any issues you may have with your ride).. The use case model gives the group a shared picture of who uses each part of the system.

3. Activity Diagrams

Our group described three main activity flows: a passenger requests a ride, a driver accepts or declines the request, and a trip is completed with payment processing. We included the decisions that affect the next step. For example, if a driver declines, the system continues searching; if payment fails, the passenger is notified and can retry or request support.

4. Begin System Context Diagram

We placed the Ride-Sharing App at the center and identifying the external entities that exchange information with it: passengers, drivers, mapping/GPS services, payment processing services, and system administrators. This gives our group a clear system boundary before breaking the application into smaller processes.

Our group will keep the original draw.io diagrams with the written explanation when the assignment requires the visual artifacts. The explanation describes the purpose of each model; it does not replace an original diagram.

Week 3: High-Level Design

1. System Context Diagram

2. High-Level Architecture Diagram

Our high-level architecture organizes the proposed system into user interfaces, an application/server layer, core services, data storage, and external integrations. The passenger and driver interfaces connect to application functions such as account management, ride management, and payment coordination. The Level 1 processes cover account management, ride requests and fare estimates, driver matching and trip tracking, payment coordination, and ratings or issue reporting. We use these diagrams to connect the requirements to the information the system receives, processes, stores, and sends to external services.

Week 4: Detailed Design

During Week 4, our group builds on the Week 3 design work and adds more detail. We are checking how information moves through the app, how database records connect, what passengers and drivers see on their screens, and how the main data fields should be defined.

1. Complete Data Flow Diagrams

Our DFD shows how passenger and driver information enters the system, how ride requests are handled, matched, trip progress is tracked, and how payment results return from the external processor. As a group, we use the DFD to check that each major process has the information it needs and that the data stores support the work.

Passenger input

Account information, pickup location, destination, and ride confirmation.

Driver matching

Checks available drivers and sends ride request details to a driver.

External services

Data storage

Stores account, ride, driver assignment, and payment status records.

Note: rectangles represent external entities, rounded process boxes represent system processes, and the database represents stored information. In formal DFDs, each arrow should be labeled with the exact data flow it carries.

Driver — Ride

Ride — Payment

Location — Ride

Database design decision to confirm: This draft uses one payment record per ride and allows a ride to have a driver assignment after matching. The group should confirm whether it needs to support multiple payment attempts or unassigned rides before treating this as final.
